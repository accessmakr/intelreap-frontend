Can You Have Two Internet Providers in One House?

How two providers work in one home, whether to use one router or two, and how to test that your backup line really works.

Yes, if both can reach your address

Yes, if both providers can reach your address. To use both on one network you need a router with two WAN connections (dual WAN), set to failover or load balancing. Two lines do not double a single download. Use the check below to compare your connections and test failover.

02 Is It Allowed?

Check three things before you order: your lease or building rules on installing a second line, each provider's terms on sharing or reselling your connection, and whether your landlord or building manager has any arrangement with a provider. In the US, a cable franchising authority may not grant an exclusive franchise (47 U.S.C. §541), so a cable company cannot hold one by law, although what is built at your address still decides which providers are available. Other countries differ, so confirm locally.

03 Can a Second Provider Reach Your Address?

In the US, the FCC's National Broadband Map lists the providers that report service at an address, with the technology and speeds they report. The data comes from providers, and the FCC provides a way to challenge it. In the UK, Ofcom's broadband and mobile coverage checker shows what services are available at a postcode, and names providers only where they have given permission. Elsewhere, ask providers directly.

A map entry is not a line installed at your door. Ask each provider whether it will install its own line, and whether it uses the same underlying network as your current provider, because two brands can share one line.

04 One Router or Two

A home router normally has one WAN port, where a single provider's modem or ONT connects. To use two providers on one network, the router must support two WAN connections, often called dual WAN or multi-WAN. Without that, the practical choices are two routers on two separate networks, or a phone hotspot as a manual backup.

05 Failover or Load Balancing

Dual-WAN routers offer two main modes. In failover, one line carries traffic and the router switches to the other when a check on the first fails, and some routers can switch back when it recovers. ASUS and TP-Link both describe failover and load balancing, and TP-Link calls failover Link Backup. The router decides a line is down with a check: ASUS uses a DNS query or a ping to the default gateway or to an address you choose.

In load balancing, the router assigns connections to the two lines by a ratio. ASUS gives 3:1 as an example, and TP-Link uses weights. Load balancing spreads many connections across both lines. It does not merge them into one faster line.

If your speed already swings before you add a second line, read why internet speed fluctuates first.

06 Why Two Lines Do Not Double One Download

RFC 8684 states that ordinary TCP communication is restricted to a single path per connection. A single download therefore uses one line at a time, while many connections at once can use both. Multipath TCP, the subject of that RFC, lets one connection use several paths, but it needs support on both ends, and an ordinary website download does not have it.

08 Setting It Up and Testing It

  1. Connect each provider's modem or ONT to its own WAN connection on the router.
  2. Open the router settings and enable dual WAN, then choose failover or load balancing.
  3. Set the check the router uses to detect a failed line, such as a DNS query or a ping to a reliable address.
  4. Read each connection with the check at the top of this page.
  5. Run the failover test while you unplug the primary line.

If you need the router's address for step 2, find your router's IP address first.

10 Costs and Side Effects

You pay for two services, and a backup line only helps if it is independent of the first. When failover switches lines, your public IP address changes, so open sessions such as VPN tunnels, remote desktop and game connections will usually drop and reconnect. Port forwarding and remote access to your home may also stop working on the backup line. The failover test shows the new address so you can see this for yourself.

Public and private addresses are explained in What Is an IP Address?

Logic

Answer the question first, then separate three things that are often confused: whether a second provider can reach you, how to connect two lines, and what two lines actually do. Give a way to check that the lines are different providers and that failover works, instead of assuming it.

Methodology

The check reads your public IP and ISP from IntelReap's identity lookup, and the network number, owner, upstream provider and mobile flag from its network lookup, then compares two readings kept in this tab. The failover test checks that you are online through public endpoints every 3 seconds for up to 5 minutes, and reads your address from IntelReap at the start, every 30 seconds, and after the connection returns. Timing is accurate to within a few seconds. A different network owner does not prove two lines are physically separate. We did not test any router or a real failover; the tool logic was exercised in a browser with simulated responses. That a changed address drops open sessions is our reasoning, and the advice to ask whether two brands share one line is general practice, not sourced. That multipath TCP needs both ends is our reading of RFC 8684. Some ASUS FAQ language versions state IPv4 limits that we saw only in Italian and Polish.

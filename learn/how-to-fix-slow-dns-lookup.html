Quick Answer

Fix slow DNS lookup by measuring first, then changing the resolver

A slow DNS lookup is a delay in turning a website name into an IP address, and it delays the whole page. Measure the lookup time first. Then switch your device or router to a faster public resolver such as 1.1.1.1, 8.8.8.8 or 9.9.9.9, flush the DNS cache, and check your VPN and router.

02 What a DNS Lookup Is and Why It Gets Slow

Every website you open has a name, and computers route by number. A DNS lookup is the step that turns the name into an IP address. Your device asks a resolver, usually run by your ISP or a public provider. If the resolver already knows the answer it replies at once. If it does not, it walks from the root servers to the top-level domain and then to the server that owns the name, as defined in RFC 1034 and RFC 1035.

Resolvers keep answers for a time set by each record, called the TTL. That is why a site you visit often loads its name quickly, and a name you have never asked for takes longer. Your browser, your operating system and often your router keep their own caches on top of that.

Lookup time matters because it comes first. Nothing else about a page can start until the name has an address, so a delay of a few hundred milliseconds is added to every new site.

03 What Counts as Slow

The numbers depend on where you are, because a lookup includes the trip to the resolver. That is why the tool above reports a recursion penalty: the time for a new name minus your fastest round trip to the resolver. It removes your distance from the resolver and leaves the work the resolver did.

Rule of thumb for the recursion penalty
PenaltyWhat it usually meansNext step
Under 100 msNormal. The resolver is doing its jobNothing to fix here
100 to 300 msNoticeable on new sitesTry another resolver
Over 300 msSlow. Often a distant, overloaded or partly failing resolverChange resolver, then retest

These ranges are this page's own rule of thumb, not an industry standard. If both resolvers in the tool look equally slow, the problem is more likely your connection than DNS.

Look at the round trip too. If it is already 150 ms or more, as it often is on mobile networks, distance dominates every lookup. A resolver run by your ISP can be closer and faster than a public one, so measure at the device with the commands below before and after any change.

04 Common Causes of Slow Lookups

Most slow lookups come from a short list of causes. A distant or overloaded resolver is the most common. A dead secondary DNS server is the classic hidden one: the device waits for the first server to time out before trying the next, so some lookups take seconds. Routers that forward DNS can add delay, VPN and security software can redirect lookups to their own servers, and a broken IPv6 path can make a device wait on an address type that never answers.

If pages also stall after the name resolves, the cause is probably not DNS. Our guide to why internet speed fluctuates covers congestion and Wi-Fi problems that look similar.

05 Fix 1: Change the DNS Resolver

Switching to a public resolver helps when your ISP's resolver is the slow part. It does not help if the public resolver is far from you, so test before and after. Three widely used ones are Cloudflare at 1.1.1.1 and 1.0.0.1, Google at 8.8.8.8 and 8.8.4.4, and Quad9 at 9.9.9.9. Set them on a single device, or set them on the router so every device benefits.

To change it on a device, open the network settings for your Wi-Fi or Ethernet connection and replace the automatic DNS servers. To change it on the router, sign in with the address from our router IP guide and look for a DNS or Internet setting.

06 Fix 2: Flush the DNS Cache

A cache can hold a bad or outdated answer. Flushing clears it. This is quick and safe, and your device simply asks again.

Flush commands
SystemCommand
Windowsipconfig /flushdns
macOSsudo dscacheutil -flushcache; sudo killall -HUP mDNSResponder
Linux (systemd-resolved)resolvectl flush-caches

Browsers keep a separate cache. Restarting the browser clears it, and Chrome also has an internal DNS page for this.

07 Fix 3: Check Your VPN, Router and Security Software

A VPN sends lookups to its own servers, so a slow VPN resolver means slow lookups even when your own is fast. Turn the VPN off and run the test again. The VPN and Proxy panel shows whether your connection is routed through one. Then restart the router, since its DNS forwarder can get stuck, and check whether security or parental-control software filters DNS.

08 Measure It the Manual Way

You can time lookups yourself. On Windows, run Measure-Command { Resolve-DnsName example.com } in PowerShell. On Mac and Linux, run dig example.com and read the Query time line. Run it twice: the second answer is cached, and the difference shows the recursion cost.

For latency to a server rather than DNS, the Live Network panel reports ping and jitter. Use both numbers together before you change anything.

Logic

Explain what a lookup is, define slow in a way that removes distance, list the causes, then give fixes in order of effect and ease. Measuring comes before and after every fix so the result is verifiable.

Methodology

The tool sends DNS-over-HTTPS queries from your browser to Cloudflare and Google. It times one warm-up, seven repeat lookups of example.com (the fastest is your round trip) and five lookups of random new names (the median is the new-name time). It cannot see your current DNS server. The penalty ranges are a rule of thumb, and results depend on your location and connection.
